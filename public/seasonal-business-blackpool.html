Running a seasonal business
in Blackpool, all year round

The shape of the Blackpool year

Blackpool has been a holiday resort for generations, and its year still follows the visitors. Spring brings the first rush around the Easter holidays. Summer is the peak. Then the Illuminations switch on at the end of the summer and stretch the season well into the autumn, with festive events extending the lights season further in recent years.

After that, the town gets quieter, and many visitor-facing businesses scale down. Every business feels this differently. Some trades get busier in winter, for example decorators, repairers and businesses that refit hotels and holiday lets between seasons.

Dates change every year, so check Visit Blackpool for the current Illuminations season rather than relying on last year's.

The pattern also varies within each week. Weekends and school holidays are busier than midweek, and the weather can change a day completely. Many seasonal businesses track takings by day as well as by month, so they can plan staffing and stock more precisely.

Is your business seasonal?

You do not have to sell rock on the Promenade to be seasonal. Look at your sales over a year, or estimate them if you are new.

  • Visitor-facing: souvenirs, attractions, entertainment, photography, mobile catering run from separate premises.
  • Supplying the visitor trade: cleaning, linen, laundry, maintenance and printing for hotels and venues.
  • Event-led: hire businesses, decorators and entertainers working around weekends and holidays.
  • Weather-led: gardening, landscaping and outdoor services.
  • Gift-led: makers and online sellers who peak before Christmas.

Knowing your own pattern lets you plan stock, staff and cash around it.

Plan cash flow for the quiet months

The most common seasonal mistake is spending peak-season income as if it will keep coming. A simple plan avoids it.

  1. Map your monthsEstimate income and costs for each month of the year, not just the total.
  2. Set aside a winter fundMove a share of busy-month takings into a separate pot for the lean months.
  3. Put tax aside tooYour tax bill reflects the whole year, including the good months.
  4. Cut flexible costs off-seasonReduce stock, hours and space you do not need in winter.
  5. Plan winter workUse quiet months for maintenance, making stock, training and marketing.

Talk to your bank or accountant before the quiet months, not during them. It is easier to arrange support while your figures look strong.

Flexible space for a seasonal business

Your space needs change across the year as much as your income. In spring you might need room to build up stock. In summer you need quick access to restock. In winter you may only need somewhere dry to keep equipment and displays.

Making the most of the peak

Preparation in the quiet months pays off when the town is busy. Have stock made or bought, kit checked and staff lined up before the rush, not during it.

  • Keep your Google Business Profile hours accurate, including seasonal changes.
  • Restock during quieter hours, using 24-hour access if you have it.
  • Track what sells each week, so next year's buying is based on facts.
  • Ask peak-season customers to follow you online so you can reach them off-season.

Seasonal staff and the rules

If you take on seasonal staff, employer duties apply from the start, even for a short summer contract. GOV.UK says employers' liability insurance is compulsory once you employ people, and you will need to run payroll and check right to work. Health and safety duties apply too.

If you sell at events or markets in the season, check licensing and food rules in good time. Our guide to selling at Blackpool markets explains what the council controls.

Using the off-season well

The quiet months are not wasted time. Many seasonal businesses do their most important work when the Promenade is empty.

  • Build stock for next season at a steady pace, rather than in a panic in spring.
  • Repair, repaint and service equipment while it is not in use.
  • Review the year's sales and drop the lines that did not earn their space.
  • Update your website, photos and Google Business Profile.
  • Try a winter income stream, such as online sales, gifts, or work for local businesses.
  • Book any training or certificates you need before the rush.

Some businesses find a second season. Makers sell at Christmas markets, event businesses take on festive parties, and maintenance trades are busy getting hotels and holiday lets ready for spring. Looking for that winter demand can turn a three-month business into a year-round one.

Before you act on this

This is general information, not legal, tax or employment advice. Event dates change each year, and employment and tax rules depend on your circumstances. Check Visit Blackpool for event dates and GOV.UK for employer and tax rules, and speak to an accountant about cash flow and tax planning.

Questions

When is Blackpool's busy season?

Broadly from spring through the summer, with the Illuminations extending it into the autumn. Exact event dates change each year, so check Visit Blackpool.

Can I rent a unit just for the season?

Our terms are rolling monthly with a one-month minimum and one month's notice, so you can rent for as long as you need, subject to availability.

Do I need insurance for a summer helper?

If you employ them, GOV.UK says employers' liability insurance is compulsory from the start, with limited exemptions. Check the GOV.UK guidance before they begin.

Can I store seasonal stock in the same unit I work from?

Yes, as long as it is a permitted item. Dry goods, displays, fabric and wooden kit are fine, while bulk flammable liquids and gas cylinders need our written agreement.

Is the Illuminations season good for all businesses?

Not necessarily. It brings visitors to the Promenade, especially in the evenings, which helps some trades much more than others. Look at your own sales from previous years rather than assuming it will be your busiest time.

Should I close completely in winter?

Some businesses do, but many find steady winter work, such as online sales, Christmas markets or work for local hotels and venues preparing for the next season. Even a little off-season income eases the strain on cash flow.
